\begin{abstract}
We develop a graph signal processing (GSP) framework for analysing atlas-based
brain connectomes and for training a geometric deep learning classifier of
glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) under an explicit smoothness prior. Structural MRI
from two complementary public sources---the healthy IXI cohort and the
University of Pennsylvania glioblastoma (UPENN-GBM) collection distributed
through The Cancer Imaging Archive---is registered to a common MNI152 reference
space and summarised over a shared $48$-node Harvard--Oxford cortical atlas
graph, so that every scan becomes a signal that can be analysed jointly in the
vertex and spectral domains. The graph Fourier transform, Dirichlet energy,
spectral centroid, spectral entropy and multiscale spectral graph wavelets are
used to characterise disease-related departures from healthy cortical
organisation. We prove that the spectral centroid and spectral entropy are
invariant to global rescaling of the signal whereas band-limited energies are
not, which identifies the former as the descriptors that remain interpretable
when intensity scales differ between cohorts. Empirically, the mean GBM
$T_1$ spectrum concentrates its energy near graph frequency $\lambda \approx
0.23$ while the healthy spectrum peaks near $\lambda \approx 0.48$ and retains
substantially more mid- and high-frequency content, yielding a lower spectral
centroid and lower spectral entropy for GBM; multiscale wavelet energy separates
the cohorts most strongly at fine scales. A graph neural network trained under a
smoothness-regularised objective---binary cross-entropy prediction loss plus a
Dirichlet energy penalty on learned node embeddings---classifies all nine
held-out subjects correctly. We show analytically that one gradient step on the
smoothness penalty is an explicit Euler step of the graph heat equation, that it
is non-expansive precisely when the effective step size does not exceed
$1/\lambda_N$, and that it acts as a low-pass filter which suppresses
high-frequency noise. We deliberately refrain from presenting the classification
result as a validated biomarker: with $28$ subjects drawn from two distinct
acquisition programmes, cohort and scanner effects cannot be separated from
disease effects, and we analyse this confound explicitly. The contribution is a
principled, interpretable and reproducible formulation in which the same
spectral object---graph Dirichlet energy---serves simultaneously as a
descriptive biomarker and as a training-time regulariser.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation}

Brain imaging data are intrinsically structured: measurements are not
independent across space but are constrained by anatomy, by functional
relationships and by long-range organisation~\cite{bullmore2009complex,
sporns2005human}. Over the past two decades, network neuroscience has
established that the brain is profitably modelled as a graph whose nodes are
anatomically defined regions and whose edges encode structural or statistical
affinity~\cite{bassett2017network,rubinov2010complex,fornito2016fundamentals}.
Classical descriptors of such networks---small-world
organisation~\cite{watts1998collective}, efficiency~\cite{latora2001efficient}
and centrality~\cite{rubinov2010complex}---characterise the topology itself.
When regional quantities are aggregated over a cortical atlas, however, each
brain scan additionally defines a \emph{signal supported on the graph nodes},
and the object of interest becomes the interaction between that signal and the
topology that carries it. This is precisely the setting of graph signal
processing (GSP)~\cite{shuman2013emerging,ortega2018graph,sandryhaila2013discrete}.

GSP generalises classical harmonic analysis from regular domains (time series,
images) to irregular graph domains by adopting the eigenvectors of the graph
Laplacian as a Fourier basis~\cite{shuman2013emerging,chung1997spectral,
mohar1991laplacian,merris1994laplacian}. The resulting machinery---the graph
Fourier transform, spectral filtering, vertex-frequency
analysis~\cite{shuman2016vertex} and spectral graph
wavelets~\cite{hammond2011wavelets,tremblay2014graph}---has proved particularly
informative in neuroimaging, where the alignment or misalignment of measured
activity with the underlying anatomical network carries functional
meaning~\cite{huang2018graph,huang2016graph,medaglia2018functional,
preti2019decoupling}. Connectome-specific harmonic
modes~\cite{atasoy2016human} and anatomically adapted graph
wavelets~\cite{behjat2015anatomically} illustrate how far the spectral viewpoint
can be pushed in this domain.

In parallel, geometric deep learning has extended convolutional architectures to
non-Euclidean domains~\cite{bronstein2017geometric,bronstein2021geometric}, with
graph convolutional networks~\cite{kipf2017semi}, localised spectral
filters~\cite{defferrard2016convolutional}, message-passing
formulations~\cite{gilmer2017neural}, attention~\cite{velickovic2018graph} and
inductive sampling~\cite{hamilton2017inductive} now forming a mature
toolbox~\cite{wu2021comprehensive,xu2019powerful}. These models have been
applied directly to population and connectome graphs for disease
prediction~\cite{parisot2018disease,ktena2018metric,li2021braingnn}. The present
work sits at the intersection of the two threads: we use spectral graph theory
both to \emph{describe} disease-related signal structure and to \emph{regularise}
a learned classifier, so that a single mathematical object governs the analysis
and the inductive bias of the model.

\subsection{Clinical Setting}

The clinical target is glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), a WHO grade 4 diffuse
astrocytic glioma that is the most common and most aggressive primary malignant
brain tumour in adults~\cite{louis2021who,wen2008malignant}. It accounts for the
largest share of malignant primary CNS tumours in population-based
registries~\cite{ostrom2023cbtrus}, and prognosis remains poor despite maximal
safe resection, radiotherapy and temozolomide
chemotherapy~\cite{stupp2005radiotherapy}. GBM is diffusely infiltrative,
molecularly heterogeneous~\cite{verhaak2010integrated} and spatially
non-uniform, so a connectome-aware representation that captures \emph{where} and
\emph{how} tumour-related signal departs from healthy organisation is well
matched to the biology of the disease.

Public imaging resources make such analyses reproducible. The IXI dataset
provides nearly $600$ healthy-subject multi-sequence MRI
volumes~\cite{ixi_dataset}, while the University of Pennsylvania glioblastoma
(UPENN-GBM) cohort distributed through The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA)
provides multi-parametric MRI with expert tumour segmentations for a large
\emph{de novo} GBM population~\cite{bakas2022university,upenngbm_tcia,
clark2013cancer}.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let
\[
G = (V,E,W)
\]
denote an undirected weighted graph with node set $V=\{1,\dots,N\}$, edge set
$E$ and symmetric weighted adjacency matrix $W \in \R^{N\times N}$ with
$W_{ij}\ge 0$. For each subject $s$, an imaging-derived regional measurement is
represented as a graph signal
\[
x^{(s)} \in \R^{N},
\]
whose $i$-th entry is the mean signal value in region $i$. A disease classifier
maps the graph signal, and optionally the graph structure, to a label
\[
y^{(s)} \in \{0,1\},
\]
with $y^{(s)}=1$ denoting GBM and $y^{(s)}=0$ denoting a healthy subject.

The central modelling principle, inherited from spectral graph theory and from
semi-supervised learning on graphs~\cite{zhou2004learning,belkin2003laplacian,
vonluxburg2007tutorial}, is that biologically meaningful graph signals are
typically \emph{piecewise smooth}: neighbouring regions take related values
except where pathology disrupts them. This motivates regularising the learned
predictive representation by graph Dirichlet energy, giving an objective that
balances prediction fidelity against structural regularity,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:main_objective_intro}
\Ltotal = \Lpred + \lambda\,\Lsmooth ,
\end{equation}
where $\Lpred$ is task-specific, $\Lsmooth$ is a Dirichlet energy penalty and
$\lambda>0$ is the regularisation weight.

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper makes the following contributions.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item We construct a shared $48$-node Harvard--Oxford atlas graph in MNI152
        space that gives every node an identical anatomical interpretation
        across a healthy cohort and a disease cohort, and therefore a single
        spectral basis for all subsequent analysis
        (Sections~\ref{sec:preprocessing}--\ref{sec:graph_construction}).
  \item We define $T_1$-intensity and tumour-burden graph signals and
        characterise disease-related departures using the graph Fourier
        transform, Dirichlet energy, spectral centroid, spectral entropy and
        multiscale spectral graph wavelets
        (Sections~\ref{sec:signals}--\ref{sec:wavelets}).
  \item We prove that the spectral centroid and spectral entropy are invariant
        under global rescaling of a graph signal, whereas band-limited energies
        are not (Proposition~\ref{prop:scale_invariance}). This is of direct
        practical consequence when cohorts are acquired on different scanners,
        and it identifies which descriptors may legitimately be compared across
        cohorts without intensity harmonisation.
  \item We show that a gradient step on the Dirichlet penalty is an explicit
        Euler step of the graph heat equation, that it is a low-pass spectral
        filter, and that it is non-expansive exactly when the effective step
        size does not exceed $1/\lambda_N$
        (Propositions~\ref{prop:gradient_diffusion} and~\ref{prop:lowpass}).
        This converts a heuristic regulariser into an analysable operator and
        yields a concrete admissible range for $\lambda$.
  \item We report spectral, wavelet and classification results on the combined
        cohort, and we analyse---rather than understate---the cohort,
        acquisition and sample-size confounds that limit what the
        classification result can support (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\runin{Graph signal processing} The spectral theory of the graph Laplacian is
classical~\cite{chung1997spectral,mohar1991laplacian,merris1994laplacian,
fiedler1973algebraic}. Its reformulation as a signal processing framework is
comparatively recent~\cite{shuman2013emerging,sandryhaila2013discrete,
ortega2018graph}, and now includes multiscale
wavelets~\cite{hammond2011wavelets,tremblay2014graph}, vertex-frequency
localisation~\cite{shuman2016vertex}, stationarity
theory~\cite{perraudin2017stationary} and methods for inferring the graph itself
from smooth observations~\cite{dong2016learning,kalofolias2016learn,
egilmez2017graph}. We take the graph as given by an anatomical atlas and
concentrate on the signal and the learning problem defined over it.

\runin{Spectral analysis of brain signals} Treating cortical measurements as
graph signals has clarified how function couples to
structure~\cite{huang2018graph,huang2016graph,medaglia2018functional}. Atasoy
et al.~\cite{atasoy2016human} interpret Laplacian eigenmodes as
connectome-specific harmonic waves; Preti and Van De
Ville~\cite{preti2019decoupling} use the split between low- and high-frequency
graph content to quantify regional structure--function decoupling; Behjat et
al.~\cite{behjat2015anatomically} design anatomically adapted graph wavelets for
group-level activation mapping. Our spectral descriptors follow this tradition,
with the addition of an explicit invariance analysis
(Proposition~\ref{prop:scale_invariance}) that we have not seen stated in this
setting.

\runin{Geometric deep learning for neuroimaging} Graph convolutional
networks~\cite{kipf2017semi,defferrard2016convolutional} have been applied to
population graphs for Autism Spectrum Disorder and Alzheimer's
disease~\cite{parisot2018disease}, to metric learning over connectivity
networks~\cite{ktena2018metric} and to interpretable region-level fMRI
models~\cite{li2021braingnn}. A recurring theme in the broader GNN literature is
that repeated neighbourhood averaging behaves as a low-pass
filter~\cite{nt2019revisiting,balcilar2021analyzing} and, taken far enough,
collapses representations---the oversmoothing
phenomenon~\cite{li2018deeper,oono2020graph,rong2020dropedge}. Our Dirichlet
penalty is an \emph{explicit} and tunable form of the same smoothing pressure;
Section~\ref{sec:oversmoothing} discusses why making it explicit is preferable
to leaving it implicit in the depth of the network.

\runin{Glioblastoma imaging} Expert-annotated public GBM collections and the
BraTS benchmark have standardised tumour sub-region
definitions~\cite{menze2015multimodal,bakas2017advancing,bakas2018identifying},
and the UPENN-GBM release adds clinical, genomic and radiomic
annotation~\cite{bakas2022university}. We use these segmentations only to define
node-wise tumour burden; we do not attempt segmentation itself.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Imaging Cohorts}
\label{sec:datasets}

Two complementary, publicly available structural MRI resources define the
healthy and disease cohorts. Using established public references on both sides
ensures that the analysis is reproducible and that node semantics are shared
across groups.

\runin{Healthy cohort (IXI)} Healthy-brain reference signals are drawn from the
IXI dataset (``Information eXtraction from Images''), a collection of nearly
$600$ MRI examinations of normal, healthy subjects acquired at three London
hospitals, comprising $T_1$-, $T_2$- and proton-density-weighted, MRA and
diffusion-weighted images~\cite{ixi_dataset}. Because IXI subjects are free of
focal pathology, their atlas-wise intensity signals provide a baseline model of
typical, smoothly varying cortical organisation against which disease-related
departures are measured.

\runin{Disease cohort (UPENN-GBM via TCIA)} Glioblastoma signals are drawn from
the University of Pennsylvania glioblastoma cohort~\cite{bakas2022university},
distributed by The Cancer Imaging Archive~\cite{clark2013cancer,upenngbm_tcia}.
UPENN-GBM provides multi-parametric MRI (pre- and post-contrast $T_1$-weighted,
$T_2$-weighted and $T_2$-FLAIR) for \emph{de novo} GBM patients together with
expert-reviewed segmentation labels demarcating the enhancing tumour, the
peritumoural oedema and the non-enhancing core. These labels follow the
annotation conventions established by the BraTS benchmark and the TCGA glioma
collections~\cite{menze2015multimodal,bakas2017advancing} and yield the
voxel-level tumour mask $M^{(s)}$ used below.

\runin{Analysed sample} The analyses reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} use
$28$ subjects in total, partitioned into $19$ training and $9$ held-out test
subjects; the held-out split comprises $6$ healthy and $3$ GBM subjects. This is
a small sample, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} examines in detail what can
and cannot be inferred from it.

\begin{remark}
The two cohorts originate from entirely separate acquisition programmes, with
different scanners, field strengths and protocols. Any between-cohort contrast
therefore confounds disease with site. We retain this design because it is the
only configuration supported by the available public data, but we treat it as a
first-order limitation throughout rather than as a nuisance detail; see
Sections~\ref{sec:results_gdl} and~\ref{sec:limitations}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Preprocessing and Spatial Registration}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

To compare subjects on a common node set, all volumes are mapped into a standard
stereotaxic space. The nonlinear MNI152 template~\cite{fonov2011unbiased} serves
as the common reference frame, and the Harvard--Oxford cortical atlas---derived
from expert manual segmentations at the Center for Morphometric
Analysis~\cite{makris2006decreased}, and conceptually related to automated gyral
parcellations of the cortex~\cite{desikan2006automated} and to other widely used
parcellations~\cite{tzourio2002automated,schaefer2018local}---supplies the ROI
labels that become graph nodes. Registration of subject images and tumour masks
to MNI space follows standard neuroimaging practice, combining intensity
inhomogeneity correction~\cite{tustison2010n4itk} with affine and nonlinear
alignment as implemented in FSL~\cite{jenkinson2012fsl,smith2004advances} and
ANTs~\cite{avants2011reproducible}.

The atlas-based construction pipeline is illustrated in
Figures~\ref{fig:mni_atlas}--\ref{fig:subject_mri_example}. The MNI152 template
provides the common reference space, the Harvard--Oxford atlas provides the ROI
labels, and subject-level MRI volumes and tumour masks are mapped into this
space so that node-wise quantities can be computed consistently across the
cohort. After registration, each atlas node receives a region-level summary
statistic, such as the mean $T_1$ intensity or the fraction of voxels covered by
tumour.

\subsection{Construction of the Connectome Graph}
\label{sec:graph_construction}

\subsubsection{Network definition}

A common atlas provides a shared node set across subjects. The Harvard--Oxford
cortical atlas defines $N=48$ cortical ROIs and hence a fixed graph topology for
all subjects, so the node set is $V=\{1,\dots,48\}$. An undirected edge
$(i,j)\in E$ is included when regions $i$ and $j$ are regarded as anatomically
or statistically connected in the shared reference graph. The graph is
represented by its weighted adjacency matrix $W$, its degree matrix $D$ and its
combinatorial Laplacian $L$:
\begin{equation}
W_{ij}=W_{ji}\ge 0,
\qquad
D_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{N} W_{ij},
\qquad
L = D-W .
\end{equation}
Because node degrees vary, the symmetric normalised
Laplacian~\cite{chung1997spectral} is also useful:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:normalized_laplacian}
\Lsym = I - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
The normalised form permits comparison across regions with different
connectivity levels, has spectrum contained in $[0,2]$, and is the operator
underlying standard graph
convolutions~\cite{kipf2017semi,defferrard2016convolutional}.

\subsubsection{Descriptive topology of the shared connectome}

Standard network-neuroscience descriptors~\cite{rubinov2010complex,
fornito2016fundamentals} summarise the topology of the shared graph.
Figure~\ref{fig:connectome_descriptive_statistics} reports the degree
distribution, the clustering coefficient distribution, the betweenness
centrality distribution, and graph renderings coloured by several node-level
attributes. If $d_i$ denotes the degree of node $i$, the average degree is
\begin{equation}
\bar d = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} d_i ,
\end{equation}
the local clustering coefficient of node $i$ is
\begin{equation}
C_i = \frac{2T_i}{d_i(d_i-1)},
\end{equation}
where $T_i$ is the number of triangles through node $i$, and betweenness
centrality quantifies how frequently a node lies on shortest paths,
\begin{equation}
B_i = \sum_{\substack{s\neq i\neq t\\ s<t}} \frac{\sigma_{st}(i)}{\sigma_{st}},
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_{st}$ the number of shortest $s$--$t$ paths and $\sigma_{st}(i)$
the number of those passing through $i$~\cite{rubinov2010complex}. These
properties are not merely descriptive: a penalty of the form $f^{\top}Lf$
weights each edge separately, so nodes with many strong incident edges
contribute disproportionately to the total regularisation. The overall strength
of the penalty is in turn governed by the Laplacian spectrum, whose smallest
non-trivial eigenvalue is the algebraic connectivity of the
graph~\cite{fiedler1973algebraic}.

\subsection{Brain Measurements as Graph Signals}
\label{sec:signals}

\subsubsection{Regional signal definition}

Given a subject-level image $I^{(s)}(\mathbf{r})$ in common space and an atlas
region $\Omega_i$, the node-wise regional summary signal is
\begin{equation}
x_i^{(s)} = \frac{1}{\mu(\Omega_i)} \int_{\Omega_i} I^{(s)}(\mathbf{r})\,d\mathbf{r},
\end{equation}
where $\mu(\Omega_i)$ is the volume of region $i$. Stacking across nodes yields
the graph signal $x^{(s)}\in\R^{N}$. Two signals are of particular interest:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item the mean $T_1$ signal $x_{\mathrm{t1}}^{(s)}$, computed from the IXI
        (healthy) and UPENN-GBM (disease) $T_1$-weighted volumes; and
  \item the tumour burden signal $x_{\mathrm{tumor}}^{(s)}$, derived from the
        UPENN-GBM expert segmentations~\cite{bakas2022university}, with
        \begin{equation}
        x^{(s)}_{\mathrm{tumor},i}
        = \frac{1}{|\Omega_i|}\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in\Omega_i} M^{(s)}(\mathbf{r}),
        \end{equation}
        where $M^{(s)}(\mathbf{r})\in\{0,1\}$ is the tumour mask.
\end{enumerate}

These two signals are of different physical types: $x_{\mathrm{t1}}$ is an
intensity in arbitrary scanner units, whereas $x_{\mathrm{tumor}}$ is a
dimensionless volume fraction in $[0,1]$. Quantitative comparisons are therefore
made \emph{within} signal type---GBM $T_1$ against healthy $T_1$---and the
tumour signal is used as a descriptive reference. Proposition
\ref{prop:scale_invariance} makes precise which descriptors are insensitive to
this distinction.

representative case, revealing a spatially non-uniform disease distribution over

GBM~\cite{louis2021who,wen2008malignant}.

Let $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{GBM}}$ and $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H}}$ denote the GBM
and healthy cohorts. Their mean signals and the induced disease contrast are
\begin{equation}
\bar x_{\mathrm{GBM}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{GBM}}|}
  \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{GBM}}} x^{(s)},
\qquad
\bar x_{\mathrm{H}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H}}|}
  \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H}}} x^{(s)},
\qquad
\Delta x = \bar x_{\mathrm{GBM}} - \bar x_{\mathrm{H}} .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Spectral Graph Analysis}
\label{sec:spectral}

\subsubsection{Graph Fourier transform}

The Laplacian $L$ is real symmetric and positive semidefinite, so by the
spectral theorem~\cite{horn2013matrix} it admits the
eigendecomposition~\cite{chung1997spectral,shuman2013emerging}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:laplacian_eigendecomp}
L = U \Lambda U^{\top},
\qquad
U = [u_1,\dots,u_N],
\qquad
\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N),
\end{equation}
with $0=\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots \le \lambda_N$ and $U$ orthogonal.
The second-smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ is the algebraic connectivity of the
graph~\cite{fiedler1973algebraic}, and the eigenvectors $u_k$ generalise the
classical Fourier harmonics to the irregular graph
domain~\cite{belkin2003laplacian,mohar1991laplacian}. The graph Fourier
transform (GFT) and its inverse are~\cite{shuman2013emerging,sandryhaila2013discrete}
\begin{equation}
\hat x = U^{\top} x ,
\qquad
x = U\hat x = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \hat x_k u_k .
\end{equation}
Low graph frequencies correspond to eigenvectors with small eigenvalues, which
vary slowly across strongly connected regions; high graph frequencies oscillate
sharply between neighbouring nodes.

\subsubsection{Dirichlet energy}

The central quantity of the paper is the graph Dirichlet
energy~\cite{shuman2013emerging,zhou2004learning},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dirichlet_energy}
\mathcal{E}(x) = x^{\top} L x
= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} W_{ij}\,(x_i-x_j)^2 .
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Vertex and spectral forms of Dirichlet energy]
\label{prop:dirichlet_forms}
For every $x\in\R^{N}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dirichlet_spectral}
\mathcal{E}(x)
= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} W_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2
= \sum_{k=1}^{N} \lambda_k \,|\hat x_k|^2 .
\end{equation}
In particular $\mathcal{E}(x)\ge 0$, with $\mathcal{E}(x)=0$ if and only if $x$
is constant on each connected component of $G$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Expanding the quadratic form and using $D_{ii}=\sum_j W_{ij}$,
\[
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} W_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2
= \sum_{i} D_{ii}x_i^2 - \sum_{i,j} W_{ij} x_i x_j
= x^{\top}(D-W)x = x^{\top} L x .
\]
Substituting $L=U\Lambda U^{\top}$ and $\hat x = U^{\top}x$ gives
$x^{\top}L x = \hat x^{\top}\Lambda\hat x = \sum_k \lambda_k|\hat x_k|^2$. Since
$\lambda_k\ge0$ the form is non-negative, and it vanishes exactly when
$W_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2=0$ for all $i,j$, that is, when $x$ is constant on each
connected component.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:dirichlet_spectral} is the formal statement that the two
readings of smoothness coincide: in the vertex domain Dirichlet energy penalises
edge-wise disagreement, and in the spectral domain it weights signal energy by
graph frequency. A signal is smooth precisely when its spectral mass sits at
small eigenvalues~\cite{belkin2003laplacian}.

\subsubsection{Spectral biomarkers}

Several scalar descriptors summarise a graph signal compactly, in the spirit of
GSP-based analysis of brain imaging~\cite{huang2018graph,huang2016graph}.

\runin{Spectral centroid} The energy-weighted mean graph frequency,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spectral_centroid}
\Cspec(x) = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N} \lambda_k |\hat x_k|^2}
                 {\sum_{k=1}^{N} |\hat x_k|^2},
\end{equation}
locates the signal in graph frequency. Larger values indicate a shift towards
less smooth components.

\runin{Spectral entropy} With normalised spectral weights
$p_k = |\hat x_k|^2 / \sum_{\ell}|\hat x_\ell|^2$, the spectral entropy is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spectral_entropy}
\Hspec(x) = -\sum_{k=1}^{N} p_k \log p_k .
\end{equation}
Larger values indicate energy distributed more evenly across graph harmonics;
$\Hspec$ attains its maximum $\log N$ for a flat spectrum and vanishes when all
energy sits in a single mode.

\runin{Band-limited energies} Let $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{low}}$ and
$\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{high}}$ index low and high graph frequencies. Then
\begin{equation}
\Elow(x) = \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{low}}} |\hat x_k|^2,
\qquad
\Ehigh(x) = \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{high}}} |\hat x_k|^2 .
\end{equation}
Larger $\Ehigh$ indicates sharper spatial variation and reduced smoothness. In
the results below, $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{high}}$ is the upper $20\%$ of the
spectrum and the energies are normalised by $\|x\|_2^2$, so that
$\Elow + \Ehigh = 1$.

The following elementary but practically important observation distinguishes the
two families of descriptors.

\begin{proposition}[Scale invariance of centroid and entropy]
\label{prop:scale_invariance}
Let $x\in\R^{N}\setminus\{0\}$ and let $c\neq 0$. Then
\begin{equation}
\Cspec(cx) = \Cspec(x),
\qquad
\Hspec(cx) = \Hspec(x),
\end{equation}
whereas the unnormalised band energies obey
$\Elow(cx)=c^{2}\Elow(x)$ and $\Ehigh(cx)=c^{2}\Ehigh(x)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The GFT is linear, so $\widehat{cx}=c\,\hat x$ and
$|\widehat{cx}_k|^2 = c^2|\hat x_k|^2$. The factor $c^2$ appears in both the
numerator and the denominator of~\eqref{eq:spectral_centroid} and cancels, so
$\Cspec$ is unchanged. Likewise the normalised weights satisfy
$p_k(cx) = c^2|\hat x_k|^2 / \sum_\ell c^2 |\hat x_\ell|^2 = p_k(x)$, so
$\Hspec$ is unchanged. The band energies are sums of $|\hat x_k|^2$ without
normalisation and therefore scale by $c^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:why_invariance_matters}
MRI intensities are expressed in arbitrary units that depend on scanner,
sequence and reconstruction, which is why intensity standardisation is a routine
preprocessing step~\cite{nyul2000new,tustison2010n4itk}. Proposition
\ref{prop:scale_invariance} shows that $\Cspec$ and $\Hspec$ are immune to a
global gain difference between cohorts, whereas raw band energies are not. The
centroid and the entropy are therefore the descriptors that may be compared
across cohorts with the fewest assumptions; the normalised energies used in
Section~\ref{sec:results} inherit the same invariance by construction, since
dividing by $\|x\|_2^2$ removes the common factor $c^2$. Invariance to a global
gain does not, however, confer invariance to the non-affine contrast differences
that distinguish scanners, and we return to this point in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Graph Wavelet Analysis}
\label{sec:wavelets}

Scalar spectral descriptors summarise the global distribution of graph frequency
content but discard localisation in the vertex domain. Spectral graph wavelets
restore it by defining multiscale filters on the Laplacian
spectrum~\cite{hammond2011wavelets,tremblay2014graph,shuman2016vertex}.

Let $g(\cdot)$ be a spectral kernel. The graph wavelet operator at scale
$\tau>0$ is~\cite{hammond2011wavelets}
\begin{equation}
T_{g}^{(\tau)} = g(\tau L) = U\,g(\tau\Lambda)\,U^{\top},
\qquad
g(\tau\Lambda) = \diag\!\big(g(\tau\lambda_1),\dots,g(\tau\lambda_N)\big),
\end{equation}
and the wavelet coefficients of a signal $x$ at scale $\tau$ are
$w^{(\tau)} = T_{g}^{(\tau)}x$. For heat-kernel diffusion wavelets one takes
$g(\tau\lambda)=e^{-\tau\lambda}$, which ties the construction directly to
diffusion geometry~\cite{coifman2006diffusion}: small $\tau$ emphasises fine
detail and large $\tau$ emphasises coarse structure. By orthogonality of $U$,
the scale-dependent wavelet energy is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wavelet_energy}
E_{\mathrm{wav}}(\tau;x) = \|w^{(\tau)}\|_2^2
= \sum_{k=1}^{N} |g(\tau\lambda_k)|^2 |\hat x_k|^2 .
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wavelet_energy} shows that wavelet energy is a reweighting of
the same spectral mass that defines $\mathcal{E}$, $\Cspec$ and $\Hspec$, with
the kernel $g(\tau\cdot)$ selecting the band under examination. This is the
bridge between the descriptive multiscale analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:results_wavelets} and the graph-regularised learning problem of
Section~\ref{sec:gdl}.

\subsection{Diffusion Dynamics and Dirichlet Smoothness}
\label{sec:diffusion}

\subsubsection{Heat diffusion on the graph}

The standard physical reading of graph smoothness is
diffusion~\cite{coifman2006diffusion}. Let $f(t)\in\R^{N}$ satisfy the graph
heat equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:heat_equation}
\frac{d f(t)}{dt} = -L f(t), \qquad f(0)=f_0 ,
\end{equation}
whose solution is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:heat_solution}
f(t) = e^{-tL} f_0 = U e^{-t\Lambda} U^{\top} f_0 .
\end{equation}
Each spectral component decays as $e^{-t\lambda_k}$, so high-frequency content
vanishes faster than low-frequency content.

\begin{lemma}[Dirichlet energy as dissipation rate]
\label{lem:dissipation}
If $f$ solves~\eqref{eq:heat_equation}, then
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}\|f(t)\|_2^2 = -2\,\mathcal{E}\big(f(t)\big) \le 0 .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating and using symmetry of $L$,
$\tfrac{d}{dt}\|f\|_2^2 = 2 f^{\top}\dot f = -2 f^{\top} L f = -2\mathcal{E}(f)$,
which is non-positive by Proposition~\ref{prop:dirichlet_forms}.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:dissipation} gives Dirichlet energy an operational meaning: it is
the instantaneous rate at which diffusion dissipates signal energy. Signals with
large Dirichlet energy contain sharp edge-wise discrepancies and smooth rapidly;
signals with small Dirichlet energy are already smooth and evolve slowly.

\subsubsection{Interpretation for brain signals}

The smoothness assumption does \emph{not} assert that disease effects are
spatially uniform. It asserts that the \emph{learned predictive representation}
should avoid node-to-node oscillation unsupported by the graph, in line with
manifold- and graph-regularised
learning~\cite{belkin2003laplacian,zhou2004learning}. The goal is not to
maximise smoothness but to minimise $\Lpred + \lambda\Lsmooth$, so that the
variation which survives is the variation the task requires.

\subsection{Geometric Deep Learning Model}
\label{sec:gdl}

\subsubsection{Model overview}

Given the graph $G=(V,E,W)$ and a subject signal $x^{(s)}$, the task is to
predict cohort membership. Following the geometric deep learning
paradigm~\cite{bronstein2017geometric,bronstein2021geometric,wu2021comprehensive},
a graph neural network maps node features to a graph-level representation and
then to a class probability,
\begin{equation}
\hat y^{(s)} = \sigma\!\big(\phi_{\mathrm{readout}}(H^{(L,s)})\big),
\end{equation}
where $H^{(L,s)}\in\R^{N\times F_L}$ is the node embedding matrix after $L$
layers, $\phi_{\mathrm{readout}}$ is a permutation-invariant graph-level pooling
function and $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid.

\subsubsection{Message passing and graph convolution}

A general message passing layer is~\cite{gilmer2017neural}
\begin{equation}
h_i^{(\ell+1)}
= \psi^{(\ell)}\!\left(
  h_i^{(\ell)},\;
  \bigoplus_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)} \phi^{(\ell)}\big(h_i^{(\ell)},h_j^{(\ell)},e_{ij}\big)
\right),
\end{equation}
where $\oplus$ is a permutation-invariant aggregation. Specialising to the
normalised-adjacency graph convolution~\cite{kipf2017semi},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gcn_layer}
H^{(\ell+1)}
= \rho\!\left(
  \widetilde D^{-1/2}\widetilde W \widetilde D^{-1/2} H^{(\ell)} \Theta^{(\ell)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde W = W + I$, $\widetilde D$ is the corresponding degree matrix,
$\Theta^{(\ell)}$ are learnable weights and $\rho$ is a pointwise nonlinearity.
Equation~\eqref{eq:gcn_layer} is a first-order localised spectral
filter~\cite{defferrard2016convolutional}, and it makes explicit how the graph
topology constrains feature mixing between neighbouring regions.

\subsubsection{Graph-level readout}

The graph-level embedding is obtained by global mean pooling,
\begin{equation}
z^{(s)} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} h_i^{(L,s)},
\end{equation}
and the predicted probability is
\begin{equation}
\hat p^{(s)} = \sigma\big(w^{\top} z^{(s)} + b\big)
= \frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta^{(s)}}},
\qquad
\eta^{(s)} = w^{\top} z^{(s)} + b .
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Training objective}

The prediction loss is binary cross-entropy over the $M$ training subjects,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bce_loss}
\Lpred
= -\frac{1}{M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}
\Big[ y^{(s)}\log \hat p^{(s)} + \big(1-y^{(s)}\big)\log\big(1-\hat p^{(s)}\big) \Big].
\end{equation}
The smoothness penalty is the matrix-valued generalisation of Dirichlet energy
over learned embeddings, extending scalar Laplacian
regularisation~\cite{zhou2004learning,belkin2003laplacian} to vector-valued node
features:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:smoothness_loss}
\Lsmooth
= \frac{1}{M}\sum_{s=1}^{M} \Tr\!\left((H^{(L,s)})^{\top} L H^{(L,s)}\right)
= \frac{1}{2M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}\sum_{i,j} W_{ij}\,
  \big\|h_i^{(L,s)} - h_j^{(L,s)}\big\|_2^2 .
\end{equation}
Neighbouring nodes with strong graph affinity are thereby encouraged to acquire
similar embeddings unless task gradients force them apart. The components of the
objective are collected in Table~\ref{tab:training_objective_summary}.

\subsubsection{The smoothness gradient is a diffusion step}

The following two results make the regulariser analysable rather than heuristic.

\begin{proposition}[Gradient flow of the penalty is graph diffusion]
\label{prop:gradient_diffusion}
Let $H\in\R^{N\times F}$ and $S(H)=\Tr(H^{\top}LH)$. Then
$\nabla_{H} S(H) = 2LH$, and a gradient descent step on $\lambda S$ with
learning rate $\eta>0$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:euler_step}
H \;\longmapsto\; H - 2\eta\lambda\, L H = (I - 2\eta\lambda L)\,H ,
\end{equation}
which is exactly the explicit Euler discretisation of the graph heat
equation~\eqref{eq:heat_equation} with time step $\Delta t = 2\eta\lambda$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Writing $S(H)=\sum_{f=1}^{F} H_{:,f}^{\top} L H_{:,f}$ and differentiating each
quadratic form with $L$ symmetric gives
$\partial S/\partial H_{:,f} = 2L H_{:,f}$, hence $\nabla_H S = 2LH$. The update
$H \mapsto H-\eta\nabla_H(\lambda S)$ is then~\eqref{eq:euler_step}. Comparing
with the forward difference $H(t+\Delta t) \approx H(t) - \Delta t\,LH(t)$
applied to~\eqref{eq:heat_equation} identifies $\Delta t = 2\eta\lambda$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Low-pass action and stability]
\label{prop:lowpass}
The update~\eqref{eq:euler_step} acts in the graph Fourier domain as the
pointwise multiplier
\begin{equation}
\hat h_k \;\longmapsto\; (1-2\eta\lambda\,\lambda_k)\,\hat h_k ,
\qquad k=1,\dots,N .
\end{equation}
Its operator norm satisfies $\|I-2\eta\lambda L\|_2 \le 1$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stability}
0 < \eta\lambda \le \frac{1}{\lambda_N},
\end{equation}
and under~\eqref{eq:stability} the multiplier is non-increasing in $\lambda_k$,
so the step attenuates high graph frequencies at least as strongly as low ones
and leaves the constant mode $\lambda_1=0$ untouched.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Applying $U^{\top}$ to~\eqref{eq:euler_step} and using $L=U\Lambda U^{\top}$
gives $U^{\top}H \mapsto (I-2\eta\lambda\Lambda)U^{\top}H$, which is the stated
pointwise multiplier. The matrix $I-2\eta\lambda L$ is symmetric with eigenvalues
$1-2\eta\lambda\lambda_k$, so its spectral norm is
$\max_k |1-2\eta\lambda\lambda_k|$. This is at most $1$ exactly when
$0\le 2\eta\lambda\lambda_k \le 2$ for every $k$, that is, when
$\eta\lambda\lambda_N \le 1$. Under this condition
$1-2\eta\lambda\lambda_k$ is non-negative and decreasing in $\lambda_k$, and
equals $1$ at $\lambda_1 = 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:lowpass} supplies a concrete admissible range for the
regularisation weight: given a learning rate $\eta$, choosing
$\lambda \le 1/(\eta\lambda_N)$ guarantees that the smoothing component of the
update cannot amplify any spectral mode. Exceeding this bound makes the
smoothing step expansive on the highest-frequency modes, so a penalty intended
to suppress oscillation would instead inject it. For the normalised Laplacian
$\Lsym$ one has $\lambda_N\le 2$~\cite{chung1997spectral}, which yields the
convenient sufficient condition $\eta\lambda\le 1/2$.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Relation to oversmoothing}
\label{sec:oversmoothing}

Proposition~\ref{prop:lowpass} also clarifies the relationship between this
penalty and the oversmoothing phenomenon. Repeated neighbourhood averaging in
deep GNNs is itself a low-pass operation~\cite{nt2019revisiting,
balcilar2021analyzing}, and iterating it drives node representations towards a
low-dimensional invariant subspace, degrading
discriminability~\cite{li2018deeper,oono2020graph}. Mitigations such as edge
dropping~\cite{rong2020dropedge} address the symptom. The formulation adopted
here differs in that the smoothing pressure is an explicit additive term with a
single interpretable coefficient $\lambda$ and a known stability
bound~\eqref{eq:stability}, rather than an implicit consequence of depth. The
network can therefore be kept shallow, and the amount of smoothing can be
selected---and reported---independently of the architecture.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:implementation}

Graph construction and topological descriptors were computed with
NetworkX~\cite{hagberg2008exploring}; atlas handling and
neuroimaging-specific operations used the nilearn/scikit-learn
stack~\cite{abraham2014machine}; numerical linear algebra, including the
Laplacian eigendecomposition in~\eqref{eq:laplacian_eigendecomp} and the matrix
exponential in~\eqref{eq:heat_solution}, used
NumPy~\cite{harris2020array} and SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}. The graph
neural network was implemented in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} with PyTorch
Geometric~\cite{fey2019fast} and trained with
Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam} for $150$ epochs on the $19$-subject training split,
with the objective~\eqref{eq:main_objective_intro} evaluated on the full graph
for every subject. Because $N=48$, the Laplacian spectrum is computed exactly
and no polynomial approximation of the spectral filters is
required~\cite{defferrard2016convolutional}.

spatially coherent perturbation on the cortical signal~\cite{louis2021who,
wen2008malignant}.

normalised energies inherit the same invariance. The ordering reported here is
therefore not an artefact of differing intensity scales, although
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} explains why it is not thereby free of all
acquisition effects.

\subsection{Multiscale Wavelet Separation}
\label{sec:results_wavelets}

Figure~\ref{fig:wavelet_boxplots} reports multiscale graph wavelet energy at
$\tau\in\{1,5,10,20\}$. The separation is large at every scale but decreases in
absolute magnitude as $\tau$ grows: healthy $T_1$ wavelet energy has median
$\approx 30$ at $\tau=1$, $\approx 7$ at $\tau=5$, $\approx 2.5$ at $\tau=10$
and below $1$ at $\tau=20$, while the tumour burden signal remains below
$\approx 0.5$ throughout. This decay is exactly what
Equation~\eqref{eq:wavelet_energy} predicts for the heat kernel: as $\tau$
increases, the multiplier $e^{-2\tau\lambda_k}$ suppresses every non-constant
mode, so all signals converge towards their graph-mean component and the
discriminative contrast migrates into the fine scales.

Fine-scale wavelet energy is the informative regime for a second reason. A
signal with large energy at small $\tau$ necessarily retains localised
irregularity on the graph, which by Proposition~\ref{prop:dirichlet_forms}
corresponds to larger Dirichlet energy and hence to a larger smoothness penalty
in the learning objective~\eqref{eq:main_objective_intro}. The descriptive
multiscale analysis and the regulariser are therefore measuring the same
property of the signal at different points in the pipeline.

Figure~\ref{fig:wavelet_coefficients_nodes} shows the corresponding node-wise
coefficients for a representative GBM tumour signal, making the vertex-domain
effect of increasing $\tau$ explicit: local peaks and abrupt node-to-node
transitions present at $\tau=1$ are progressively suppressed until only broad
graph structure survives.

\subsection{Geometric Deep Learning Classification}
\label{sec:results_gdl}

Figure~\ref{fig:geometric_deep_learning} reports the training behaviour and
held-out performance of the smoothness-regularised graph neural network over
$150$ epochs. Three patterns are visible.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item The total objective decreases monotonically and almost identically on
        the training and test splits, from $\approx 0.68$ to $\approx 0.11$ and
        $\approx 0.09$ respectively, with the test curve running slightly below
        the training curve throughout.
  \item Classification accuracy improves in discrete steps, as expected for
        small splits: training accuracy passes through $12/19$, $14/19$, $16/19$
        and $18/19$ before reaching $19/19$ near epoch $105$, while test accuracy
        moves from $6/9$ through $8/9$ to $9/9$ near epoch $62$.
  \item The prediction loss falls by more than an order of magnitude while the
        smoothness penalty \emph{rises} from $\approx 5$ to $\approx 37$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter4_assets/geometric-deep-learning.png}
    \caption{Geometric deep learning results over $150$ training epochs.
    Top left: total objective on the training and test splits. Top right: the
    task-specific prediction loss against the Dirichlet smoothness penalty.
    Bottom left: training and test accuracy. Bottom right: the test confusion
    matrix, with all $6$ healthy and all $3$ GBM held-out subjects classified
    correctly.}
    \label{fig:geometric_deep_learning}
\end{figure}

The third pattern is the most informative, and it is not a contradiction. The
optimiser minimises the \emph{sum} in~\eqref{eq:main_objective_intro}, so an
increase in $\Lsmooth$ is admissible whenever it buys a larger decrease in
$\Lpred$. That the plotted total objective remains close to the prediction loss
throughout, despite the penalty growing to $\approx 37$, indicates that
$\lambda$ is small in this configuration: the regulariser is shaping the
embedding geometry without dominating the objective. The behaviour is the
intended one. The penalty does not prohibit task-relevant graph variation; it
prices it, so that node-to-node variation is purchased only where it earns
discriminative value~\cite{zhou2004learning}. Read through
Proposition~\ref{prop:lowpass}, training is a competition between a task gradient
that injects high-frequency structure into $H^{(L,s)}$ and a diffusion step that
removes it, and the converged embedding is the fixed point of that competition.

The test confusion matrix shows $6/6$ healthy and $3/3$ GBM subjects correctly
classified. The PCA projection of the learned subject embeddings
(Figure~\ref{fig:pca_projection}) shows the corresponding geometry: the GBM
subjects spread over a broad region spanning roughly $-3.3$ to $-0.7$ along the
first principal component, whereas the healthy subjects collapse into an
extremely tight cluster near $\mathrm{PC}_1\approx 1.15$ with negligible
within-class spread.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{chapter4_assets/pca-projection.png}
    \caption{PCA projection of the learned subject-level graph embeddings. GBM
    subjects spread broadly along the first principal component while healthy
    subjects collapse to a near-degenerate cluster. The asymmetry is discussed
    in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.}
    \label{fig:pca_projection}
\end{figure}

This asymmetry warrants caution rather than celebration. A class that collapses
to a near-point in embedding space is the signature of a feature the network
finds trivially and uniformly satisfied across that class. Combined with the
fact that test accuracy saturates well before the training accuracy does---the
model classifies all held-out subjects correctly while still misclassifying
training subjects---the pattern is more consistent with an easily separable,
globally consistent cohort difference than with a subtle pathological signature
recovered from individual anatomy. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} identifies the
most plausible such difference.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Principal Findings}

The descriptive analyses establish that the cohorts differ in how signal energy
is distributed over graph frequencies and graph scales:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item GBM $T_1$ signals concentrate their spectral energy at lower graph
        frequencies ($\lambda\approx0.23$) than healthy $T_1$ signals
        ($\lambda\approx0.48$);
  \item healthy $T_1$ signals exhibit a higher spectral centroid, higher
        spectral entropy and greater normalised high-frequency energy;
  \item normalised low-frequency energy is saturated and non-discriminative,
        so the usable contrast lies in the high-frequency residual;
  \item multiscale wavelet energy separates the groups at every scale examined,
        most strongly in absolute terms at $\tau=1$;
  \item disease effects are localised over specific atlas nodes and subnetworks
        rather than distributed uniformly.
\end{itemize}

Taken together these support the use of graph-structured regularisation: the
graph carries biologically meaningful spatial
organisation~\cite{bullmore2009complex,bassett2017network}, and the spectrum
provides an interpretable basis for quantifying departures from typical
organisation~\cite{huang2018graph,medaglia2018functional,preti2019decoupling}.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Work}

This framework joins two literatures that are usually pursued separately. GSP has
been used to analyse functional and structural brain imaging by treating cortical
measurements as signals more or less aligned with the anatomical
graph~\cite{huang2018graph,huang2016graph,medaglia2018functional,
atasoy2016human,preti2019decoupling}; geometric deep learning has produced
powerful graph classifiers, including for neuroimaging
cohorts~\cite{bronstein2017geometric,kipf2017semi,defferrard2016convolutional,
gilmer2017neural,parisot2018disease,ktena2018metric,li2021braingnn}. The specific
contribution here is to make the \emph{same} spectral object---graph Dirichlet
energy---serve as both the descriptive biomarker (through the GFT and spectral
graph wavelets~\cite{shuman2013emerging,hammond2011wavelets}) and the
training-time regulariser, which Propositions~\ref{prop:gradient_diffusion}
and~\ref{prop:lowpass} identify with Laplacian-based semi-supervised
smoothing~\cite{zhou2004learning,belkin2003laplacian} and with heat diffusion on
the graph~\cite{coifman2006diffusion}. The model's inductive bias is thereby
stated explicitly, measured in the same units as the descriptive analysis, and
equipped with a stability condition, rather than being left implicit in the
architecture.

\subsection{Stability, Robustness and Denoising}

The design pairs a fixed shared graph with subject-varying signals, which confers
three stability properties:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \emph{Common node semantics.} Every node denotes the same atlas region
        across subjects, because both cohorts are registered to MNI152 and
        parcellated with Harvard--Oxford~\cite{fonov2011unbiased,makris2006decreased}.
  \item \emph{Shared spectral basis.} A single eigenbasis $U$ serves all GFT
        analyses, so spectral coefficients are directly
        comparable~\cite{chung1997spectral}.
  \item \emph{Regularised learning.} The Dirichlet penalty limits sensitivity to
        noisy subject-specific node fluctuations~\cite{belkin2003laplacian}.
\end{enumerate}

The third property admits a precise statement. Suppose the observed signal is
$x = x^{\star} + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon$ an additive perturbation. Since
$L$ is symmetric,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:noise_decomposition}
\mathcal{E}(x)
= \mathcal{E}(x^{\star}) + 2(x^{\star})^{\top} L \varepsilon + \mathcal{E}(\varepsilon),
\qquad
\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_k |\hat\varepsilon_k|^2 .
\end{equation}
Noise concentrated at high graph frequencies therefore inflates the Dirichlet
energy in proportion to $\lambda_k$, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:lowpass} the
smoothing step attenuates precisely those modes most strongly. The penalty thus
functions as a denoiser as well as a structural prior~\cite{shuman2013emerging}.
The cross term $2(x^{\star})^{\top}L\varepsilon$ vanishes in expectation when
$\varepsilon$ is zero-mean and independent of $x^{\star}$, in which case
$\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(x)] = \mathcal{E}(x^{\star}) + \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\varepsilon)]$
and the penalty is inflated by a strictly positive, frequency-weighted noise
term.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The classification result must be read with its design in mind, and we state the
constraints explicitly rather than in passing.

\runin{Cohort and acquisition confound} This is the principal limitation. The
healthy and disease signals originate from two unrelated acquisition programmes:
IXI was collected at three London hospitals, UPENN-GBM at the University of
Pennsylvania, with different scanners, field strengths, sequences and
reconstruction pipelines. Cohort membership is therefore perfectly confounded
with site, and no analysis of this dataset can attribute the observed separation
to pathology rather than to acquisition. The near-degenerate healthy cluster in
Figure~\ref{fig:pca_projection}, and the saturation of test accuracy before
training accuracy, are both consistent with the network exploiting a globally
consistent, easily detected cohort-level difference. Proposition
\ref{prop:scale_invariance} removes one specific channel for this
confound---a global intensity gain---but scanner differences are not confined to
a global gain, and no invariance argument can neutralise them in general.
Resolving this requires either healthy controls imaged under the UPENN-GBM
protocol or explicit intensity harmonisation~\cite{nyul2000new,tustison2010n4itk}
with a site-shuffled negative control.

\runin{Sample size and evaluation protocol} The analysed sample comprises $28$
subjects with a single $19/9$ split, so the reported accuracy is a point estimate
on nine test subjects. The binomial confidence interval around $9/9$ is wide, and
a single split provides no estimate of variance across partitions. Small-sample
neuroimaging results are known to be unstable and to overstate effect
sizes~\cite{button2013power,marek2022reproducible}, and accuracy estimated
without nested cross-validation is a recognised source of optimistic
bias~\cite{varoquaux2017assessing,poldrack2017scanning}. Accordingly, the
classification experiment should be read as a demonstration that the framework is
implementable and internally consistent, not as evidence of a validated
biomarker. Repeated stratified cross-validation with a permutation test for the
null would be the minimum required to make a performance claim.

\runin{Heterogeneous signal types} The spectral comparison involves a continuous
intensity signal and a binary-derived volume fraction. We have therefore
foregrounded the like-for-like GBM $T_1$ versus healthy $T_1$ contrast
(Figure~\ref{fig:t1_descriptor_boxes}) and treated the tumour signal as a
descriptive reference, but conclusions that mix the two signal types remain
weaker than those drawn within a type.

\runin{Fixed graph and limited modality} The shared graph fixes one topology for
all subjects, which buys a common spectral basis at the cost of ignoring
subject-specific connectivity~\cite{sporns2005human,rubinov2010complex}. Only
$T_1$ and the binary tumour mask are used, leaving the post-contrast $T_1$, $T_2$
and FLAIR sequences and the graded sub-region labels of UPENN-GBM
unexploited~\cite{bakas2022university,menze2015multimodal}.

\subsection{Future Work}

Four directions follow directly. First, and most importantly, the cohort confound
should be addressed by sourcing healthy controls under a matched protocol or by
harmonising intensities and verifying that separation does not survive label
permutation. Second, the evaluation should move to repeated stratified
cross-validation with permutation-based significance
testing~\cite{varoquaux2017assessing}, and the cohort should be enlarged, for
example by combining UPENN-GBM with the TCGA glioma
collections~\cite{bakas2017advancing,bakas2018identifying}, to enable
cross-site validation. Third, the fixed atlas graph could be replaced by
subject-specific structural or functional connectomes, or learned from the data
under smoothness assumptions~\cite{dong2016learning,kalofolias2016learn,
egilmez2017graph}, trading the shared spectral basis for subject specificity.
Fourth, richer spectral filters---higher-order Chebyshev
polynomials~\cite{defferrard2016convolutional}, attention-weighted
aggregation~\cite{velickovic2018graph} or anatomically adapted
wavelets~\cite{behjat2015anatomically}---could sharpen both the descriptive
biomarkers and the learned representations, and the stability
bound~\eqref{eq:stability} offers a principled starting point for selecting
$\lambda$ rather than tuning it blindly.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper develops a graph signal processing pipeline for atlas-based
connectome analysis of glioblastoma multiforme, built on public, reproducible
imaging resources~\cite{ixi_dataset,bakas2022university,upenngbm_tcia}. Its
contributions are a shared $48$-node Harvard--Oxford graph in MNI152 space that
gives both cohorts identical node semantics and a single spectral basis; graph
signal definitions for $T_1$ intensity and tumour burden; spectral biomarkers
together with a proof that the centroid and entropy are invariant to global
rescaling while band energies are not; a multiscale graph wavelet analysis;
a diffusion-theoretic account of the Dirichlet penalty, including its
identification with an explicit Euler step of the graph heat equation and the
resulting stability bound $\eta\lambda\le1/\lambda_N$; and a geometric deep
learning classifier trained under that penalty.

The central claim is that connectome-aware analysis is valuable both
descriptively and predictively, and that a single object serves both roles.
Descriptively, the graph Fourier basis and graph wavelets localise where
disease-related information sits across graph frequencies and
scales~\cite{shuman2013emerging,hammond2011wavelets,huang2018graph}.
Predictively, the same Laplacian supports the regularised objective
$\Ltotal = \Lpred + \lambda\Lsmooth$, which is principled because it encodes the
assumption that neighbouring regions should carry related latent representations
except where the task benefits from preserving edge-wise
contrast~\cite{zhou2004learning,belkin2003laplacian}, and analysable because
that penalty is a low-pass diffusion operator with a known admissible range for
$\lambda$.

The empirical classification result is reported as a feasibility demonstration
rather than as a validated biomarker: with $28$ subjects drawn from two distinct
acquisition programmes, cohort effects cannot be separated from disease effects,
and resolving that confound is the necessary next step. The mathematical
framework, by contrast, is independent of this particular dataset, and we expect
the combination of spectral analysis, graph wavelets and smoothness-regularised
geometric learning~\cite{bronstein2017geometric,bronstein2021geometric} to
remain a coherent basis for connectome-aware disease modelling as larger and
better-matched cohorts become available.
